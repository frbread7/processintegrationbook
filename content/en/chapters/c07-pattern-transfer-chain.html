A mask, its transferred feature, and the surface inherited by the next module are one integration decision.

Decision question and objectives

Decision question: can the selected pattern definition and transfer sequence deliver the geometry and interface that the downstream module actually requires, across the stated process window?

  • Trace a critical feature from layout intent through mask definition, transfer, and planarization.
  • Separate overlay, printed-feature, etch-transfer, and topography contributions instead of treating them as one “CD error.”
  • Specify downstream acceptance evidence and identify what a measured mismatch cannot establish by itself.

One feature crosses several contracts

The feature begins as a design intent: location, width, spacing, enclosure, and electrical role. Patterning converts that intent into a mask target and an exposure/overlay problem. Transfer then maps the resist or hard-mask contour into the target film; the resulting bottom width, sidewall, residue, and damage can differ from the image at the resist top. Planarization changes the surface on which later alignment and focus depend, and may alter local thickness or expose/retain material differently across density patterns.

These are different measurands. Overlay describes relative placement between patterned levels; critical-dimension (CD) measurement describes a width under a specified metrology method; edge placement combines placement and edge-location errors for a particular pair of features. A satisfactory average CD does not establish correct overlay, profile, or contact enclosure. Likewise, an overlay correction cannot repair a systematically biased transferred profile. See the specialist LithoBook transfer chapter and overlay chapter for standalone patterning methods; this chapter focuses on the handoffs.

Etch selectivity, mask consumption, aspect-ratio-dependent behavior, and endpoint determine whether the target receives the intended contour without unacceptable stop-layer loss. CMP then sees a patterned material stack, not an abstract “flat wafer”: layout density, feature height, material contrast, and pattern history affect erosion, dishing, and residual topography. Those outcomes feed the next lithography level and can alter line/contact interfaces. The EtchBook selectivity chapter and ProcessBook CMP chapter cover those unit mechanisms.

Build the error and evidence budget

A useful review partitions the chain into (1) mask-to-mask placement, (2) printed edge location, (3) transfer bias/profile, and (4) post-planarization topography and thickness. For independent, zero-mean random terms expressed in a common edge-placement metric, a first screening estimate is σtotal = √(σoverlay2 + σprint2 + σtransfer2). This root-sum-square is not valid for correlated terms, systematic offsets, unlike measurands, or non-Gaussian tails; keep signed means and covariance where known, and do not infer a process capability from this expression alone.

Illustrative assumption: a fictional contact must enclose an underlying landing feature by 8 nm on each side. A proposed level shows 3 nm (1σ) placement variation and 2 nm (1σ) edge-location variation, treated as independent and centered solely for a screening estimate. Their combined standard deviation is √13 ≈ 3.6 nm per relative edge. That number does not answer whether the minimum enclosure is met: the nominal enclosure, both edges, systematic shifts, tails, etch bias, and metrology uncertainty still need a consistent geometric model and measured distributions. An integration review should first ask whether the measurement represents the post-etch contact edge and whether CMP topography changes the alignment reference.

Engineering inference: if a contact-resistance shift follows local pattern density while overlay remains stable, density-dependent transfer or planarization is a candidate, not a conclusion. Discriminate it with matched-density structures, pre/post-CMP topography and thickness measurements, and contact geometry/profile evidence. Use the validated WAT extraction procedures only after confirming the contact structures and measurement conditions; see WATBook.

Worked integration choice

Illustrative assumption: a dense array and an isolated monitor both pass a mask-level CD target, yet the dense array shows a wider post-transfer distribution and later contact opens. Option A tightens the exposure target. Option B changes the hard-mask/etch handoff and adds density-matched post-CMP profile and contact-chain monitors. Option A addresses only one possible contributor and may shift isolated structures; Option B tests where the divergence enters and captures the surface inherited by contact patterning. Choose B as the next learning split, not as a production fix: hold the mask constant, compare transfer/profile and CMP state by density, then inspect contact continuity. If those measurements do not discriminate the hypotheses, retain both and design a controlled follow-up.

This case is fictional and contains no process recipe or foundry data. The evidence label is Illustrative assumption; the choice of discriminating measurements is an Engineering inference.

Knowledge check

A level-to-level short appears after CMP. Overlay metrology is within its target. Is overlay ruled out, and what should be checked next?

No. The sampled overlay metric may not describe the affected local edges, and it does not measure transfer profile or topography. Check location-correlated edge placement, post-transfer profile, local CMP topography/thickness, and the short's physical cross-section or electrical localization before assigning cause.
