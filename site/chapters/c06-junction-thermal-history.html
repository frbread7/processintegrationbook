Chapter 06

Junction evolution under a shared thermal history

Learning objectives

  • Accumulate the diffusion exposure from several process holds without reducing a non-isothermal flow to its peak temperature.
  • Interpret the result of a one-dimensional Gaussian-profile estimate with correct units.
  • Identify physical effects that make a simple equilibrium Arrhenius model unreliable after implant or at a moving interface.

A shared history is an integration constraint

A junction is affected by the thermal sequence that follows dopant introduction, not by one anneal considered in isolation. A later oxidation, contact treatment, or material cure can add diffusion exposure; the same step can also serve another module's purpose. Integration therefore asks two linked questions: what profile change does the accumulated history permit, and what other device or material constraints limit a change to that history?

The thermal-history laboratory below is a deliberately narrow calculation. It combines diffusion from a selected reflected-Gaussian initial profile. Its inputs are synthetic teaching values except for a historically reported Arrhenius pair whose original textbook provenance was not independently confirmed. The result is a scenario estimate, not a production recipe or a calibrated silicon prediction.

Accumulate diffusivity over time

For a dilute equilibrium species in a homogeneous region, an Arrhenius approximation is

D(T) = D₀ exp(−Eₐ / (kB T))

Here D₀ is in cm²/s, Eₐ in eV, kB = 8.617333262 × 10⁻⁵ eV/K, and T must be absolute temperature in kelvin. Celsius input is converted by adding 273.15. For holds indexed by i, the one-dimensional diffusion integral is

Θ = Σᵢ D(Tᵢ) tᵢ

Θ has units of cm². Under Fickian diffusion with concentration-independent D(T) at each temperature, each Gaussian component with initial standard deviation σ₀ broadens to σf² = σ₀² + 2Θ. The factor of two is the one-dimensional variance relation; it is not a three-dimensional radial distance.

Two-hold check: this selected Arrhenius pair gives D(950 °C) ≈ 4.2119 × 10⁻¹⁵ cm²/s and D(1050 °C) ≈ 5.0343 × 10⁻¹⁴ cm²/s. A synthetic 200 s hold at 950 °C contributes 8.4239 × 10⁻¹³ cm²; a 20 s hold at 1050 °C contributes 1.0069 × 10⁻¹² cm². Their sum, 1.8492 × 10⁻¹² cm², is unchanged when the two holds are swapped within this model. This follows because a shared linear diffusion operator depends only on integrated exposure. It does not imply real process-order equivalence: if one hold oxidizes the surface, injects point defects, changes the phase, or shifts the boundary condition, the assumed operator and initial/interface state change. Compare orders with measurements or a validated state-dependent model before treating them as interchangeable.

From a profile to a threshold crossing

The laboratory now treats Q as the dose retained in the silicon half-space x ≥ 0. It assumes a reflecting, zero-flux surface, ∂C/∂x = 0 at x = 0, and an idealized symmetric image of the initial Gaussian. Its parameter Rp is the Gaussian-lobe center: far from the surface it approximates the projected range, but near the surface it is not the mean depth of a measured implant. With a uniform opposite-type background NB, the assumed chemical concentration is

C(x) = Q / (√(2π) σf) · [exp(−(x − Rp)² / (2σf²)) + exp(−(x + Rp)² / (2σf²))], x ≥ 0

The two image terms give ∫₀∞ C(x) dx = Q and cancel the surface gradient. Fick's equation ∂C/∂t = D(t) ∂²C/∂x² broadens both terms by σf² = σ₀² + 2Θ under the stated shared-material, concentration-independent model. This is the half-line Neumann image construction, not a model of dopant segregation or loss through a real interface. Ivrii, Partial Differential Equations, heat equation on a half-line.

Use Q in cm⁻², Rp and σf in cm inside the equation, and NB in cm⁻³; the page accepts and reports lengths in nanometers. The reported peak is the maximum of this two-term profile on x ≥ 0, which can move to the surface. The right-side crossing x* is found from C(x*) = NB on the descending side when the peak exceeds NB; there is no general single-Gaussian closed form. It is not a measured or electrically activated junction depth: activation, compensation, realistic implant asymmetry, surface exchange, and device electrostatics are omitted.

Reference calculation and interpretation

The calculator uses D₀ = 0.76 cm²/s and Eₐ = 3.46 eV. Esser, Hobart, and Kub print this boron-in-silicon pair in an illustrative diffusion calculation and cite S. M. Sze's edited VLSI Technology (1983). Mostafa and Medraj's review also prints the equation and cites W. J. Scotten's Diffusion in Silicon (2000). Neither historical source's original page or table was independently verified, so the pair's original provenance remains unresolved. We use the values as reported in accessible publications: a historical dilute-equilibrium parameterization, not a universal constant or process-calibrated value. Esser et al. (2004, p. G387); Mostafa and Medraj (2017, boron–silicon section).

Diffusion studies bound how far to generalize this simple pair. Bracht et al. report boron, phosphorus, and arsenic diffusion in silicon isotope multilayers from 850–1100 °C and analyze native-point-defect contributions; their abstract describes boron diffusion as mainly self-interstitial-mediated. Sadigh et al. use first-principles calculations, molecular dynamics, and kinetic Monte Carlo to study an interstitialcy mechanism. These are experimental and atomistic context, respectively; neither calibrates this calculator. Bracht et al. (2007); Sadigh et al. (1999).

Illustrative check: with σ₀ = 20 nm, Rp = 80 nm, Q = 2 × 10¹³ cm⁻², NB = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³, and one 1050 °C, 20 s hold, the model gives D ≈ 5.0343 × 10⁻¹⁴ cm²/s, σf ≈ 24.5229 nm, and x* ≈ 163.413 nm. The independent reference test reproduces these values from the displayed equations. All profile inputs in this example are synthetic.

What this model cannot decide

  • It assumes one diffusivity law and one material region throughout every hold. Do not combine holds that change the dopant, ambient, phase, or dominant mechanism under one parameter set.
  • It omits implantation damage and transient-enhanced diffusion, point-defect injection during oxidation, concentration- and charge-state-dependent transport, clustering, activation, segregation, moving interfaces, and two-dimensional geometry.
  • Fair (1975) reports concentration-dependent profiles for high-concentration boron diffusions above about 2 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ in neutral ambients. The lab uses a modeled peak above this approximate range only as a literature-informed caution trigger; its synthetic scenario does not establish Fair's ambient or experimental conditions. This is not a universal physical cutoff, and the calculation continues to use constant dilute diffusivity on either side. Fair (1975).
  • Temperature ramps are not modeled; breaking a ramp into holds is an approximation that requires an adequately resolved temperature history.
  • The laboratory's profile crossing is not a process window or a junction measurement. A real decision needs calibrated profiles and electrical structures under the target stack and sequence.

Use this estimate to ask which later thermal steps deserve attention. Use TCADBook for detailed diffusion/implant physics, WATBook for measurement conditions and uncertainty, and the relevant integration team data to test the actual device.

Implant damage, activation, and junction evidence

Synthetic integration comparison: two implant/anneal splits have the same modeled Θ and similar measured total-dopant depth profiles, yet their sheet resistance and reverse-biased diode leakage differ. The reflected diffusion calculator predicts a chemical-profile width under one selected law; it does not predict electrically active dopant, carrier mobility, residual damage, or junction leakage. Therefore, equal calculated σf cannot qualify the two splits as equivalent.

  1. Compare dose- and depth-calibrated chemical profiles, for example with SIMS, on matched wafers. A profile difference beyond measurement uncertainty challenges the shared diffusion law, implant initial state, or boundary assumption; implant-induced point defects can cause transient enhanced diffusion.
  2. Measure sheet resistance with comparable geometry and use a carrier-sensitive measurement such as Hall where valid. Sheet resistance alone mixes active carrier density and mobility, so a shift does not uniquely identify activation. Keep contact/access resistance and measurement uncertainty separate.
  3. Compare defect-sensitive structural evidence at the relevant depth and reverse-bias leakage on matched junction structures. Structural methods have detection limits; leakage can also move with edge geometry, surface/interface traps, and contamination. Use controls or a targeted split before attributing a change to residual implant damage.

Disposition: reconcile the differences among the chemical, electrical, and defect observables with each method's uncertainty and remaining alternative explanations; compare each split separately against predeclared device and reliability limits. An accepted split need not have identical observables to the other, and no fictional result here establishes acceptance. Fiory et al. report separate SIMS depth, sheet resistance/Hall activation, and diode-leakage observables for shallow implanted silicon junctions (2002 paper, pp. 999–1001); a silicon implant-damage model describes point-defect populations relevant to TED (IEEE abstract). Their reported conditions and outcomes are not assigned to this fictional split.

Knowledge check

Two holds use the same dopant and material model. The second hold is cooler but much longer. Which conclusion is justified?

For this model the accumulated variance change is twice the sum of D(Tᵢ)tᵢ. Arrhenius dependence makes a mean temperature generally wrong, and changes in mechanism or interfaces invalidate simply summing one coefficient.
